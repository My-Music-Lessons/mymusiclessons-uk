ONE CLEAR NEXT STEP

From a first conversation to music in a room

  1. 01

    One conversation

    You name the school. We find out what is already running there, what the funding allows, and what we could put on next term. It costs you a sentence, and most of our partnerships began with one.

  2. 02

    A room and a rotating slot

    You give us a room and a slot that rotates through the week. Nothing else on your timetable changes. The rotation is the part that matters: it is what stops the same child missing the same subject every week, which is the single commonest reason a school stops instrumental teaching after a year.

  3. 03

    We staff it, and we cover it

    Our teachers arrive. They teach one-to-one, in small groups and in bands of up to five. When one of them is ill we send cover, because they travel a fixed weekly round and somebody else on it is already nearby. Your office is never asked to find a replacement, and a covered week is a taught week rather than a refunded one.

  4. 04

    The records come back to you

    Registers, practice notes and safeguarding evidence, held by us and open to your team whenever you ask. A practice note goes home after every lesson, so a parent knows what happened without anybody in your office having to tell them.

WHAT HEADS ASK FIRST

The questions we are actually asked

In the order they tend to arrive in, and answered the way we would answer them on the phone.

Who pays for a place?
A place has one payer and never two. Either the school funds it, and that child pays nothing, or the family pays a flat monthly amount - never part of each. Many schools fund a set number of places and the other families pay for theirs. Which applies depends on the school and its funding, not on who asks first.
What does it cost the school to run?
A room, a slot, and nothing else. No cover to arrange, no invoices to chase, no instruments to buy or insure, and no safeguarding paperwork to hold. We bill families directly where families are paying, so your office is not in the middle of it.
How many children are in a lesson?
Between one and five. One-to-one where that is what a child needs, small groups where learning alongside somebody else is the point, and bands or ensembles of up to five - which is where a child stops practising and starts playing with other people.
What can they learn?
Piano, Guitar, Drums, Singing, Ukulele. Five instruments, taught properly, rather than a longer list taught by whoever is free. A school agrees its own mix with us, and it is usually settled by what the children ask for in the first term rather than by us.
What about safeguarding?
Every teacher who comes into your building is checked before they do, and we hold the evidence rather than asking you to. Registers and practice notes are kept with it, so if you are ever asked to account for what happened in a room on a Tuesday, the answer exists and you can see it.
How long does it take to start?
The conversation is short and the answer is usually a term. What takes the time is not us: it is your timetable, your funding cycle and the point in the year when adding something is sensible rather than disruptive. We would rather start in September with the right room than in March with the wrong one.
